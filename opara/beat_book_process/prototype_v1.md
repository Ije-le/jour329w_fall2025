# Reporter's Guide: Public Safety

**Eastern Shore Public‑Safety Beat Book (Nov 2023 – Oct 2025)**  
*Your go‑to narrative guide for covering police, fire, courts, emergency‑management and the community‑safety stories that shape Maryland’s Eastern Shore.*

---

## 1.  Emergency Services & Fire Departments  

### Who you’ll hear from most often  

The voice of the fire and EMS world comes from a handful of chiefs, fire‑company presidents and state‑level emergency‑management staff.  

* **Chief Graham Norbury** (Oxford Volunteer Fire Company) and **Chief Tim Kearns** are the public faces of the fire‑house that responded to the 2024 “Helene” storm, coordinating the donation of gear to Tennessee and leading the town’s burn‑ban enforcement.  
* **Chief Brian LeCates**, Director of Talbot County Emergency Services, runs the county‑wide coordination center that fields every 911 call, from a drowned toddler in Greensboro to a crane‑fire at a Delmarva Power sub‑station.  
* **Chief Geneva Schaffle**, Talbot County Emergency‑Management Division Chief, issues the monthly “Preparedness Proclamation” and leads the annual SKYWARN training that draws dozens of volunteers each spring.  
* **Chief Eric Kellner** (Oxford Police) and **Chief Alan Lowrey** (Easton Police) are the two officials who sit on the inter‑agency “Complete Streets” steering committee, a project that blends traffic‑engineering with fire‑response needs.  
* **Chief Lenox Trams** (Greensboro) was promoted in January 2024 and has become the go‑to source for the town’s K‑9 program, which is being rebuilt after the state‑wide cannabis‑legalization funding freeze.  

When a fire breaks out, the first line of quote is usually a fire chief or a volunteer company president (e.g., **President Jeff Gottleib** of Tilghman Island VFD). For large‑scale incidents you’ll also hear from **Maryland State Fire Marshal** officials who certify investigations, and from **Talbot Paramedic Foundation** director **Wayne Dyott**, who supplies the community‑wide AED network.

### Institutions that matter  

* **Volunteer Fire Companies** – Tilghman Island VFD (centennial in 2025), Oxford Fire Co., St. Michael’s VFD, and the new EMS/Paramedic station in Cordova. These groups are funded largely by local fundraising drives, the Maryland State Fire Marshal’s grant program, and occasional private gifts (the $300 k Preston Automotive grant for a first‑responder training campus in Easton).  
* **Talbot County Department of Emergency Services** – the hub for 911 dispatch, regional mutual‑aid, and the “Burn‑Ban” ordinance that shuts down open‑flame burning for 30‑day periods during drought.  
* **Maryland State Police Aviation Unit** – provides the air‑support for seaplane crashes (the 2024 Bay Bridge Airport incident) and assists in large‑area searches after floods.  
* **Delmarva Power** – its sub‑station crane fire in September 2024 triggered a coordinated response that highlighted the need for clear utility‑emergency protocols.  

### Recurring issues that shape the beat  

1. **Burn‑ban enforcement and drought** – Talbot County’s 30‑day bans, renewed each summer, create tension with local farmers and equipment operators who argue the restrictions hurt productivity. The fire chief’s press releases and the county manager’s statements give you two sides of the same story.  
2. **K‑9 program rebuilding** – After Maryland legalized cannabis, funding for police K‑9 units dried up. Talbot Sheriff **Joe Gamble** and Easton Lt. Mike Larrimore have been lobbying the Maryland Police K‑9 Foundation for new dogs, creating a multi‑year narrative about resource gaps and community safety.  
3. **Volunteer‑fire recruitment** – The Tilghman Island centennial celebration in July 2025 was billed as a “junior‑firefighter competition” to attract new members. Similar drives appear each spring; reporters can follow the pipeline from youth program to certified firefighter.  
4. **Infrastructure‑related emergencies** – The crane fire at the Delmarva Power sub‑station, the 2024 Bay Bridge Airport seaplane crash, and the 2025 crane accident on a water‑system valve in Greensboro all illustrate how construction, utilities and transportation intersect with fire‑department response.  

### Geographic quirks  

* **Oxford** – The historic town’s narrow streets and its three‑way stop at Tilghman & Bank make traffic‑flow a constant safety conversation. The fire department there works closely with the town council on “Complete Streets” data gathering.  
* **Greensboro** – A water‑system sabotage case (chlorine‑regulator tampering) in July 2024 turned a routine fire‑call into a criminal investigation, pulling in the town manager, the police chief, and the county sheriff.  
* **Cambridge** – The city’s waterfront location drives frequent “boat‑related drownings” and “Safe‑Boating Week” columns, while the fire department also handles the occasional waterfront fire.  
* **Talbot County** – A sprawling, rural jurisdiction where volunteer fire companies are the norm; the county’s emergency‑services budget is a barometer for how well the region can respond to storms, wildfires and motor‑vehicle crashes.

## 2.  Law Enforcement & Community Policing  

### Core voices  

* **Chief Justin Todd** (Cambridge PD) and **Chief Shane Hinson** (Cambridge Captain of Community Policing) are the public‑facing officials for “Shop with a Cop,” National Night Out, and the town‑hall meetings that discuss protest‑permit procedures.  
* **Chief Alan Lowrey** (Easton PD) has become the focal point for the “permit‑process debate” after the “No Kings” protests were slated for the town’s central square. He repeatedly stresses the need for a formal permitting system that balances First‑Amendment rights with public‑safety staffing.  
* **Sheriff James Phillips** (Dorchester) and **Sheriff Joe Gamble** (Talbot) are the two sheriffs most often quoted on ICE‑related arrests, juvenile‑detention capacity, and the recent demand for the resignation of **Secretary Vincent Schiraldi** of the Department of Juvenile Services.  
* **Sheriff Donnie Baker** (Caroline) and **Sheriff Donald Baker** (Caroline – different person, same county) are the sheriffs who negotiated the Ridgely Police‑Department MOU, a cost‑sharing agreement that keeps the town’s police presence alive after the department’s dissolution.  
* **Captain Antoine Patton** (Cambridge PD) and **Captain Greg McCray** (Cambridge Lieutenant) are the officers who led the investigation into the water‑system sabotage in Greensboro, illustrating how local police collaborate with county sheriffs on technical crimes.  

### Institutions that shape the story  

* **Town Police Departments** – Cambridge, Easton, Federalsburg, Ridgely (now under a sheriff‑county MOU). Each department’s size and budget dictate the scope of community‑policing programs and the frequency of “citizen police academy” sessions.  
* **County Sheriff’s Offices** – Dorchester, Talbot, Caroline, Kent, Queen Anne’s. The sheriffs provide both law‑enforcement services to unincorporated areas and supplemental support to towns that have disbanded their police forces (e.g., Ridgely).  
* **Maryland State Police (MSP)** – Provides highway patrol, aviation support, and forensic assistance for homicides and large‑scale crashes (the 2024 Bay Bridge Airport crash).  
* **Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE)** – Its presence on the Eastern Shore surged after the 2024 Trump‑era policy shift, prompting a series of “No Kings” protests and a wave of community‑outreach videos that went viral on Facebook.  

### Ongoing story threads  

1. **Protest‑permit policy** – The “No Kings” protests (June 2025) sparked a town‑council debate in Easton and Cambridge about whether a formal permit is required for any large gathering. The police chiefs argue for a permit to allocate resources; civil‑rights groups cite First‑Amendment concerns. Follow the council minutes and any subsequent ordinance changes.  
2. **Sheriff‑Police MOUs** – Ridgely’s 2025 renewal of its MOU with Caroline County (cost $493 k / yr) is a live case study of how small towns maintain public safety without a stand‑alone police budget. The negotiation documents and the community’s reaction make for a great explanatory piece.  
3. **ICE‑related tension** – The July 2024 video of an ICE officer in Cambridge, the August 2025 “No Kings” protest, and the September 2025 detention of Pastor Daniel Espinal (later released) illustrate a broader national‑policy story playing out locally. Interview the ICE liaison, the local pastor, and the sheriffs to capture the human side.  
4. **K‑9 program restoration** – Talbot Sheriff Gamble and Easton Lt. Larrimore are lobbying for new K‑9 units after funding dried up in 2023. Their quarterly updates on training pipelines, grant applications, and community‑outreach (K‑9 demonstrations at schools) give a recurring human‑interest angle.  

### Geographic focus  

* **Cambridge** – The city’s downtown is a hot‑spot for protests, shop‑with‑a‑cop events, and a recent homicide‑investigation that led to the 2025 trial of **Kenneth Bradley**.  
* **Easton** – The seat of Talbot County, Easton is where the “Complete Streets” workshops are held, where the town council debates protest permits, and where the AED network is expanding.  
* **Ridgely** – A small town that dissolved its police department in 2023 and now relies on a sheriff‑county MOU; the community’s feelings about the change are mixed and provide a fertile ground for a feature on rural policing.  
* **Greensboro** – The site of a water‑system sabotage that required coordination between the town police, Caroline County Sheriff’s Office, and the Maryland Department of the Environment.

## 3.  Courts, Prosecution & the Justice System  

### Who tells the courtroom story  

* **State’s Attorney Amanda Leonard** (Dorchester) – lead prosecutor in the 2024 Cambridge kidnapping‑murder case (Bradley/Britt).  
* **State’s Attorney Lance Richardson** (Queen Anne’s) – often quoted on juvenile‑detention capacity and on the “tough‑on‑crime” reforms pushed by the Department of Juvenile Services.  
* **State’s Attorney Joseph Coale** (Talbot) – handles the high‑profile arson case on SunnySide Energy trucks (2025) and the child‑porn investigation involving **Darian Braun**.  
* **Judge William Jones** (Dorchester Circuit) – presided over the sentencing of **Tramelle Williams** (two life‑without‑parole terms) and the 2024 sentencing of a Bay Bridge Airport crash driver.  
* **Judge Philip Cronan** (Talbot Circuit) – issued the plea deal for **Kenneth Bradley** (Cambridge kidnapping‑murder).  

### Institutions that shape outcomes  

* **Circuit Courts** – Dorchester, Talbot, Queen Anne’s, Caroline, Kent. Each handles felony trials, while the District Courts manage misdemeanors, traffic violations, and juvenile matters.  
* **Department of Juvenile Services (DJS)** – under Secretary **Vincent Schiraldi**; the subject of a coordinated demand for resignation from three sheriffs (Dorchester, Talbot, Caroline) in October 2024.  
* **Police Accountability Board (Talbot County)** – created in 2023, it reviews use‑of‑force complaints and the “Year‑and‑a‑Day” rule that forces prosecutors to bring charges within a year of an alleged incident.  

### Patterns that repeat  

1. **Homicide and kidnapping trials** – The Eastern Shore has seen three major murder cases in the past two years (Williams, Bradley, and the 2024 Cambridge kidnapping). Each trial offers an opportunity to explore victim impact, community healing, and the role of plea bargains.  
2. **Child‑exploitation prosecutions** – The 2024 indictment of **Darian Braun** for possession of child‑porn material, and the 2025 seizure of illegal sexual‑abuse material in Queen Anne’s, highlight the growing partnership between local sheriffs, the FBI’s Internet Crimes Division, and state prosecutors.  
3. **Juvenile‑detention capacity battles** – Sheriffs in Dorchester, Talbot and Caroline have repeatedly testified before the General Assembly, warning that the state’s juvenile‑detention wing is at 98 % capacity. This fuels a broader debate about “rehabilitation vs. incarceration.”  

### Geography of the courts  

* **Dorchester County** – Handles the Cambridge homicide and the 2024 Bay Bridge Airport crash; the courthouse is a frequent venue for high‑profile cases that attract regional media.  
* **Talbot County** – Home to the Police Accountability Board, the Juvenile‑Services protest, and the 2025 Ridgely MOU hearing.  
* **Queen Anne’s County** – Often the venue for drug‑trafficking and child‑exploitation cases that cross county lines, reflecting the county’s position on the transportation corridor (US 301).

## 4.  Government, Policy & Budgeting  

### The people who decide the money  

* **Mayor Cheryl Lewis** (Oxford) – spearheads traffic‑safety studies and the three‑way stop at Tilghman & Bank.  
* **Council President Chuck Callahan** (Talbot) – signed the 2023 “National Preparedness Month” proclamation and pushes for Vision‑Zero grant applications.  
* **County Manager Clay Stamp** (Talbot) – negotiates the $300 k Preston Automotive grant and the $493 k annual cost of the Ridgely MOU.  
* **Town Engineer Rick Van Emburgh** (Easton) – leads the “Complete Streets” data‑gathering workshops that blend traffic engineering with pedestrian safety.  
* **State Secretary of Juvenile Services Vincent Schiraldi** – the focal point of the sheriffs’ resignation demand in October 2024.  

### Institutions that wield the purse strings  

* **Talbot County Council** – controls the county’s emergency‑services budget, the Vision‑Zero grant allocation, and the burn‑ban ordinance funding.  
* **MD Department of Transportation (MDOT) – State Highway Administration (SHA)** – responsible for the Route 662 closure for the new hospital, the Brookletts Ave. traffic‑light timing adjustments, and the “Complete Streets” grant program.  
* **Maryland Department of Emergency Management** – issues the annual preparedness proclamations and coordinates storm‑response drills with local fire departments.  

### Recurring policy debates  

1. **Traffic‑safety vs. historic preservation** – Oxford’s three‑way stop generated a clash between preservationists who want to keep the historic streetscape and traffic engineers pushing for Vision‑Zero‑style redesigns.  
2. **Burn‑ban extensions** – Each summer Talbot County Council debates whether to extend the 30‑day ban. The debate pits the fire chief’s public‑safety data against farm owners’ economic concerns.  
3. **Juvenile‑services reform** – The sheriffs’ letter to Governor Moore demanding Secretary Schiraldi’s resignation is a flashpoint for a statewide conversation about mandatory minimums for juvenile offenders.  
4. **Protest permitting** – Easton’s town council is considering a new ordinance that would require any gathering of more than 50 people to obtain a permit three days in advance. The proposal has drawn both praise for safety planning and criticism for potentially chilling free speech.  

### Geographic nuances  

* **Oxford** – The town’s narrow historic streets make any traffic‑engineering change a community‑wide conversation.  
* **Talbot County** – Rural, with a dispersed network of volunteer fire companies; budget decisions have a direct impact on response times across a large geographic area.  
* **Caroline County** – Home to the Ridgely MOU, its county commissioners must balance the cost of policing multiple small towns with limited tax revenue.

## 5.  Human‑Trafficking, Victim Services & Community Advocacy  

### Key advocates you’ll meet  

* **Susan Ahlstrom** – Human‑Trafficking Navigator for **For All Seasons**; she runs the “Regional Navigator” program that trains law‑enforcement officers and social‑service workers to spot trafficking indicators.  
* **Robin Davenport** – Executive Director of **CASA of the Mid‑Shore**; her office is the voice for children navigating the court system, especially in cases involving abuse or neglect.  
* **Heather Grant** – Director of **Talbot Thrive**, a nonprofit that runs the bike‑safety and “Stop‑the‑Bleed” campaigns, often collaborating with fire departments on community‑first‑aid training.  

### Institutions that support victims  

* **For All Seasons** – Provides crisis counseling, a 24‑hour hotline, and the regional navigator program that partners with police and hospitals.  
* **CASA of the Mid‑Shore** – Court‑appointed advocates for children, often quoted in juvenile‑detention capacity stories.  
* **Talbot Paramedic Foundation** – Funds AED placements, “Stop‑the‑Bleed” kits, and community CPR training.  

### Recurrent themes  

* **Under‑reporting** – Many trafficking victims are hesitant to approach law enforcement; Susan Ahlstrom’s workshops often highlight the need for culturally‑competent outreach.  
* **Child‑advocacy overload** – CASA volunteers report a 30 % increase in caseloads after the 2024 surge in juvenile‑detention placements, a trend that dovetails with the sheriffs’ calls for DJS reform.  
* **Community‑first‑aid** – The Talbot Thrive AED network, now over 40 units strong, is a story‑seed for a “life‑saved” human‑interest series.  

### Geographic considerations  

* **Cambridge & Easton** – Higher concentration of trafficking hot‑spots due to proximity to the Port of Cambridge and the rail line that runs through Easton.  
* **Talbot County’s rural townships** – Victims often travel long distances to reach services, making transportation a barrier that reporters can explore.

## 6.  Infrastructure, Economic Support & Seasonal Issues  

### Who handles the nuts‑and‑bolts  

* **Preston Automotive Group** – Donated $300 k to build a first‑responder training campus in Easton, a project that will host fire, EMS and police joint drills.  
* **Delmarva Power** – Its sub‑station crane fire in September 2024 forced a coordinated outage response; the utility’s public‑relations director provides technical explanations for the public.  
* **MDOT SHA** – Manages the Route 662 closure for the new Shore Regional Health hospital, and the Brookletts Ave. traffic‑light timing adjustments that prompted a resident‑petition in February 2025.  

### Seasonal patterns that drive stories  

* **Summer drought** – Triggers the 30‑day burn‑ban, increased wildfire risk, and a rise in water‑system sabotage attempts (the Greensboro chlorine‑regulator incident).  
* **Fall hurricane season** – Brings “Complete Streets” workshops (August 2025) and post‑storm infrastructure repair funding requests.  
* **Winter ice‑season** – Maryland State Police trooper **Ian Rola** leads the deer‑season safety campaign each December, an annual story that can be tied to traffic‑safety data.  

### Ongoing issues  

* **Route 662 closure** – The temporary detour has spurred complaints from farmers who claim the detour adds 15 minutes to grain‑truck routes. The county’s road‑budget hearings are a source of quotes and data.  
* **Bridge‑traffic warnings** – MDOT’s “Bridge‑Traffic Advisory” on US 301 is updated after each storm, and the alerts are often the first line of information for commuters.  
* **AED placement** – The Talbot Paramedic Foundation’s push for “AEDs on every corner” has resulted in 12 new units in 2024; each placement can be tied to a life‑saved story.  

### Geographic focus  

* **Easton** – The hub of the new first‑responder campus, the site of the “Complete Streets” data‑gathering sessions, and the town where the 2025 protest‑permit ordinance is being debated.  
* **Talbot County** – The area where Route 662, the burn‑ban, and the $300 k Preston Automotive grant intersect.  
* **Greensboro** – The location of the water‑system sabotage and the site of a new fire‑house capital‑campaign (planned 2026).

### How to use this toolbox  

Pick one angle, map out the key players (using the “Key People” sections above), locate the most recent data (court dockets, agency press releases, council minutes), and schedule a sit‑down with the primary source. Most stories on the Eastern Shore bloom when you can blend a **human‑interest hook** (a farmer, a survivor, a teen) with the **policy or procedural backdrop** (burn‑ban, Vision‑Zero, juvenile‑detention reform).

## 9.  Putting It All Together – A Typical Reporting Workflow  

1. **Scan the daily 911 log** (Talbot County EMS, Cambridge PD, Easton PD). Note any incidents that involve multiple agencies – those are often the ones that generate the richest narratives.  
2. **Check the council‑meeting calendar** for each town (Easton, Oxford, Ridgely, Greensboro). When a meeting is scheduled, skim the agenda for items like “traffic‑study,” “burn‑ban,” or “MOU renewal.”  
3. **Pull the latest court docket** for any pending homicide, kidnapping, or juvenile‑detention case. Look for plea‑deal filings, sentencing hearings, or motions to dismiss.  
4. **Reach out to the identified “Key People”** early in the story‑building process. A quick email to Chief Norbury or Sheriff Gamble can secure a quote before the news cycle moves on.  
5. **Add the community angle** – contact the local nonprofit (For All Seasons, CASA, Talbot Thrive) to ask how the incident impacts the people they serve.  
6. **Map the geography** – use a simple GIS overlay (roads, fire stations, police precincts) to illustrate response times or coverage gaps. Readers on the Eastern Shore love to see their town highlighted on a map.  
7. **Tie it to a seasonal hook** – if it’s July, frame the story around the burn‑ban; if it’s October, link it to the “No Kings” protest or the upcoming Halloween‑night‑watch drill.

### Final Thought  

The Eastern Shore’s public‑safety beat is a **living tapestry** of tiny towns, volunteer fire companies, a handful of sheriffs and police chiefs, and a community that leans on its first responders for everything from wildfire prevention to civil‑rights protests. By keeping the **people**, **institutions**, **issues**, and **places** in clear view, you’ll be able to turn a routine incident report into a story that tells the **human side of safety**—the same side that matters most to the readers of the Star‑Democrat, the Eastern Shore Gazette, and the regional public‑affairs outlets.  

Welcome to the beat. The stories are waiting on the next call, the next council meeting, and the next courtroom door. Happy reporting!
